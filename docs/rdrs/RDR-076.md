# RDR-076: Extended Verification Add-On (Defense in Depth) — Business Review Package and Rubric

> Extends RDR-075. This document defines the business-facing review package and
> verification-selection rubric; it does not replace the host policies,
> obligation model, coordinator contract, or evidence requirements proposed by
> RDR-075.

## Problem Statement

RDR-075 supplies the typed obligations, six-category selection rubric,
coordinator scheduling, claim-addressed evidence, obligation-level coverage,
finding classes, and three entry paths that let a verification plan be
mechanically executed and mechanically validated. None of that answers the
business-facing question: *"can a person who did not author this change read
the verification story and tell whether the obligation is satisfied, what is
not yet proven, and what risk remains acceptable?"*

The gap is the missing business review surface. The plan may select the right
layers and run them at the right times, yet remain effectively unauditable to
an operator or reviewer who arrives after the fact. RDR-075's coordinator
mechanically aggregates evidence and coverage, but the result is an
implementation-facing report: structured results, finding classes, and
claim-linked evidence. A business reviewer needs the same material assembled
into a single, versioned, decision-ready package — one that exposes the
business obligation, the selected layers and the rationale, the
approvals, the unresolved items, and the final completion decision, all
linked to the same content digests.

The gap is the wrong default for verification selection. RDR-075 enumerates
six risk categories, which conflates the obligation's risk with the obligation
itself. Two obligations can be similarly risky and still demand materially
different layers (a payment limit and a discoverability nudge). RDR-076
expands the selection decision from six risk categories to nine
complementary layers keyed to the obligation property being verified, so the
business question drives the plan and the plan's rationale is part of the
review package.

The gap is the missing independent audit path. A reviewer who arrives after
release needs a way to ask "is the evidence still sufficient?" without
re-running the original obligation authoring. RDR-075's three entry paths
share the same coordinator, but only the shared-design and bootstrap paths
are exercisable from a freshly authored obligation. The independent audit path
needs an explicit workflow and rubric so a non-author can detect a
plausible-but-incomplete verification story.

The gap is the missing upgrade of the selection rubric to a defense-in-depth
posture. Six categories are a floor; a defensible plan often needs more than
one layer per failure mode and more than one verifier per layer. The
business-facing rubric in RDR-076 makes the *complementarity* of layers
explicit, so a reviewer can challenge a plan that satisfies the rubric's
category check while still leaving a failure mode uncovered.

## Context

### What RDR-075 supplies

Per the umbrella issue and the
[2026-10-12 implementation-status audit](audit-report-2026-10-12-rdr-075.md),
RDR-075 (held for human review in
[PR #4364](https://github.com/viamin/paid/pull/4364), not yet merged) proposes
the following foundation:

- **Typed obligations** — a `BusinessObligation` model distinct from
  `ChangeIntent`, with a stable identity, a content digest, an owner, an
  actor, a desired outcome, constraints, and a status.
- **Verification coordinator** — a substrate that schedules verifiers through
  the existing `agent_harness` run infrastructure, preserves mandatory host
  gates (CI, security, code review, branch protection, approved-design
  conformance), and never lets a successful add-on check silently waive
  policy.
- **Six-category selection rubric** — obligation risk is bucketed into six
  categories; each category maps to a default layer set.
- **Claim-addressed evidence** — evidence records are content-addressed by
  obligation claim digest, package revision, source revision, policy
  snapshot, producer identity, and validity window.
- **Obligation-level coverage** — every obligation carries a `MET`,
  `NOT MET`, or `UNKNOWN` coverage state with the contributing verifiers and
  freshness recorded.
- **Four finding classes** — `missing_evidence`, `stale_evidence`,
  `drifted_obligation`, and `failed_verification`, each with class-distinct
  Inbox actions.
- **Three entry paths** — shared design, independent audit, and
  bootstrap/implementation.
- **A rollout guard** — a `layered_verification_coordinator` feature flag,
  default off, preserving the host-policy contract.

RDR-075 is the substrate. RDR-076 does not add a coordinator, a new layer,
or a new obligation type. It adds the business review surface and rewrites
the selection rubric from six risk categories to nine complementary layers.

### What this RDR adds

RDR-076 proposes four additions, each layered on RDR-075:

1. A business-facing review package — a versioned, immutable-at-review
   artifact that assembles obligation register, change boundary, verification
   plan, approval record, evidence index, coverage report, findings, and
   completion decision into a single, reviewable document. The package is
   available through all three RDR-075 entry paths.
2. A nine-layer selection rubric — keyed to the obligation *property* being
   verified (business-process formalization, verification planning, engineering
   translation, linkage/freshness, behavioral testing, formal verification,
   mutation testing, implementation/conformance, independent semantic audit)
   rather than to a risk category. Multiple layers are expected where they
   answer different failure modes.
3. An independent audit workflow — explicit steps for a reviewer who arrives
   after release, with the rubric, the evidence index, and the coverage report
   as the inputs.
4. A nine-layer coordinator contract — each layer has the same typed boundary
   (input digests, output structure, failure classification, invalidation
   conditions, approval role) so the package can treat evidence uniformly and
   replace one verifier with another without re-authoring the package.

### What this RDR explicitly does not change

- It does not propose a new obligation type or modify the obligation model.
- It does not propose new host policies or weaken the host-policy contract.
  The nine-layer rubric still names the host gates and still cannot waive
  them.
- It does not propose a new coordinator. The RDR-075 coordinator is reused;
  the package is an *output* the coordinator produces in addition to the
  per-run result it already produces.
- It does not propose a new feature flag. The
  `layered_verification_coordinator` flag (or its successor) added by the
  RDR-075 rollout guard governs the package as well.
- It does not claim that RDR-075 has merged. The 2026-10-12 readiness audit
  records that no `RDR-075*.md` file exists under `docs/rdrs/`, that no
  `BusinessObligation` model or `business_obligations` table exists, that no
  coordinator or rubric service exists, and that the umbrella #4365 must
  remain open until Phases 1–4 (#4366–#4369) and the closeout (#4370) ship.

### Why the additions are defense in depth

The risk in any verification system is that a single layer can be satisfied
without the obligation actually being met. A green unit-test run can fail to
exercise the business rule; a passing mutation score can fail to surface the
ambiguity the obligation was hiding; a signed audit can rubber-stamp an
incomplete plan. RDR-076's defense-in-depth posture is the nine complementary
layers plus the package-plus-rubric-plus-audit triple, with the host gates
preserved as mandatory. No single layer can clear the obligation on its own;
no layer can waive the host gates; no layer can convert missing evidence
into `MET`; and the package plus the rubric plus the independent audit make
the complementarity visible to a non-author.

### Key discoveries

**A mechanical coordinator is not a reviewable story.** RDR-075's
coordinator produces structured results and coverage states that an
implementation engineer can read. A business reviewer — the release
authority, the security lead, the auditor — needs the *same* material
assembled into a single document with a digest, an approval record, and a
completion decision. The package is that document; it is not new
infrastructure, it is a structured *output* the coordinator produces.

**Six categories are a floor, not a defense.** Six risk categories can
collapse into a single layer set per category, which means a plan can be
"complete" by the rubric yet leave a failure mode uncovered. Nine
complementary layers, selected by obligation *property*, make the
complementarity explicit and make a weak plan visible to a reviewer.

**A non-author cannot reliably audit without an explicit workflow.** A
reviewer who arrives after release cannot rely on the original author's
narrative. The independent audit path needs explicit steps: identify the
affected obligation set, reconstruct or import the last approved package,
check layer selection against the rubric, run invalidated or sampled
checks, and synthesize coverage. The audit must not rewrite the
obligation to fit observed code.

**The add-on must not weaken host gates.** The package's completion decision
must surface the host gates — CI, security, code review, branch
protection, approved-design conformance — as a separate, mandatory
section. A successful add-on check cannot waive any of them, and the
package cannot report `MET` while a host gate is failing. This is the same
contract RDR-075 records, repeated here to make the defense explicit at
the review surface.

**A successful run is not a complete plan.** A layer may run green and still
fail the obligation (e.g., a behavioral test that exercises the wrong
path). The package treats evidence as one input among many; it does not
collapse "all layers green" into "obligation met" without an explicit
human semantic audit, and it does not collapse "human audit passed" into
"obligation met" without all layers having produced current, claim-linked
evidence.

## Proposed Solution

Adopt a business-facing review package for every verification plan produced
by the layered-verification coordinator. The package makes the business
obligation, selected verification layers, evidence, approvals, unresolved
questions, and completion decision reviewable by a person who did not author
the change.

The package is available through three entry paths:

1. **Shared design path** — a business obligation is introduced or changed in
   an approved design before implementation begins.
2. **Independent audit path** — a reviewer starts from an existing obligation,
   implementation, or release decision and asks whether the evidence is still
   sufficient.
3. **Bootstrap/implementation path** — an implementation or change request
   enters verification without a complete review package and must be brought
   to a reviewable state before completion.

RDR-075 supplies the layered-verification foundation: typed obligations,
selection, coordinator scheduling, claim-addressed evidence, obligation-level
coverage, finding classes, and these same three entry paths. RDR-076 adds the
business review surface and expands the selection decision from six categories
to nine complementary layers. It is defense in depth: a layer may strengthen a
decision, but it may not silently waive a mandatory host policy or convert
missing evidence into success.

### 1. Shared design entry path — obligation-first business review

This path is the default for a new feature, a material behavior change, or a
change to an existing business obligation. It begins before implementation and
leaves an approved package that later paths can reuse.

#### 1.1 Business-facing review package

The package is a versioned, immutable-at-review artifact with links to its
source claims and evidence. It should be readable without opening source code.
Its minimum contents are:

| Package part | Required content | Business question answered |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Obligation register | Obligation ID, actor, desired outcome, constraints, priority, owner, and status | What must be true, for whom, and who owns the decision? |
| Change boundary | In-scope behavior, explicit non-goals, affected surfaces, and assumptions | What is changing and what is deliberately unchanged? |
| Verification plan | Selected layers, rationale, required evidence, order, and stop conditions | How will we know the obligation is satisfied? |
| Approval record | Approver identity/role, decision, timestamp, package digest, and conditions | Who accepted the plan and under what conditions? |
| Evidence index | Claim digests, producer, environment, timestamps, validity window, and links | Can another reviewer find and trust the proof? |
| Coverage report | `MET`, `NOT MET`, or `UNKNOWN` per obligation, with confidence and rationale | Which obligations are proven, disproven, or unresolved? |
| Findings and exceptions | Finding class, severity, owner, due date, disposition, and compensating control | What remains risky and what happens next? |
| Completion decision | Release/merge decision, reviewer, rationale, and package digest | Is this review complete, and is the result permitted to proceed? |

The package is complete only when every in-scope obligation has a terminal
coverage state, every required approval is recorded, all mandatory layers have
valid evidence, and no unresolved item is being represented as `MET`.

#### 1.2 Workflow and handoffs

| Order | Actor | Trigger and action | Handoff / approval |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Business owner | Creates or changes an obligation and names the outcome, constraints, and acceptance boundary | Hands obligation to product/design owner |
| 2 | Product/design owner | Confirms scope, actors, non-goals, and decision authority | Approval that the obligation is ready for planning |
| 3 | Verification coordinator | Normalizes the obligation, identifies affected claims, and proposes layers using the rubric | Hands a draft plan to engineering and an independent reviewer |
| 4 | Engineering owner | Translates obligations into design elements, tests, conformance checks, and implementation seams | Confirms the plan is implementable; does not approve its own evidence as independent audit |
| 5 | Evidence producers | Execute planned checks and record immutable evidence linked to claim digests | Handoff to coordinator for synthesis |
| 6 | Independent reviewer | Reviews package completeness, layer rationale, exceptions, and evidence provenance | Approves, rejects, or requests clarification |
| 7 | Business owner / release authority | Decides whether the remaining risk is acceptable within policy | Records final completion or an explicit exception |

The ordering is intentional: formalize the business meaning before selecting
tests, translate it before implementing, and audit the assembled evidence only
after the evidence-producing work has run. The coordinator may schedule work in
parallel after the obligation and plan are approved, but it may not skip a
predecessor's required artifact.

#### 1.3 Decision rules

1. An obligation must have one stable identity and a content digest. Textually
   similar obligations are not merged merely because they use the same words.
2. A selected layer must name the property it verifies, its expected evidence,
   its owner, and its invalidation conditions.
3. Mandatory host policies remain mandatory. A successful add-on check cannot
   waive CI, security, code review, branch protection, or approved-design
   conformance.
4. `MET` requires current, claim-linked evidence from every required layer.
   `NOT MET` means evidence contradicts the obligation. `UNKNOWN` means a
   required check did not run, is stale, is inconclusive, or lacks provenance.
5. A failed verification finding cannot be cleared by rerunning a different
   prompt or by changing the selected verifier without an explicit plan
   revision and fresh approval.
6. A layer may be omitted only when the coordinator records a justified
   inapplicability decision. Cost, schedule pressure, or reviewer preference
   alone is not sufficient justification.
7. An exception requires an owner, expiry/review date, scope, compensating
   control, and approval from the authority that owns the affected risk.
8. Evidence is invalid when its input digest, environment, policy snapshot, or
   validity window cannot be established. Invalid evidence produces `UNKNOWN`.

#### 1.4 Acceptance scenarios

| Class | Scenario | Expected result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Permitted | A payment-limit obligation selects behavioral tests, mutation testing, implementation conformance, and an independent semantic audit; all evidence is current and linked | Package may reach `MET` and proceed through the normal release approval |
| Permitted | A UI accessibility obligation omits formal verification because the coordinator records it as inapplicable to the chosen runtime representation | Omission is permitted when the rationale and stronger applicable layers are approved |
| Forbidden | A green unit-test run is used to declare a security or authorization obligation satisfied without policy/conformance evidence | Coverage remains `UNKNOWN` or `NOT MET`; completion is blocked |
| Forbidden | An engineer marks a failed verification as complete by changing the prompt, verifier, or claim wording | Original finding remains; plan revision and independent review are required |
| Boundary | A low-risk copy change affects no executable behavior but changes a customer-facing obligation description | Linkage/freshness and independent semantic review may suffice; coordinator records why deeper layers are unnecessary |
| Boundary | A database migration changes a limit-enforcing query and also changes its business wording | Treat as a material obligation change: formalize, reselect layers, and invalidate stale evidence |
| Unresolved | A required external integration is unavailable and the test cannot establish the result | Coverage is `UNKNOWN`, with a recovery owner and retry/exception path; it is not `MET` |
| Unresolved | Mutation testing survives a mutant because the expected behavior is ambiguous | Open a clarification finding, revise the obligation/test contract, and rerun affected layers |

#### 1.5 Exceptional paths and recovery

- **Missing owner or approver**: pause at the coordinator, assign the missing
  role, and do not schedule a completion decision.
- **Plan changes after approval**: increment the plan revision, calculate
  impacted claims, invalidate affected evidence, and obtain approval again.
- **Stale or corrupted evidence**: quarantine the record, preserve the original
  failure metadata, rerun the producer, and retain the audit trail.
- **Transient infrastructure failure**: classify the failure, retry through the
  owning runner's bounded retry policy, and report `UNKNOWN` while unresolved.
- **Contradictory evidence**: retain both records, create a conflict finding,
  and require independent semantic adjudication; never select the favorable
  result by default.
- **Emergency release**: use the existing emergency authority only with an
  explicit exception, compensating control, expiry, and post-release review.

### 2. Independent audit entry path — evidence and decision review

This path starts with an implementation, release, incident, customer report,
or periodic review rather than a newly authored design. It is deliberately
independent of the original author so that the package can detect a plausible
but incomplete verification story.

#### 2.1 Audit workflow

1. The auditor identifies the affected obligation set and records the source
   revision, environment, and review question.
2. The coordinator reconstructs or imports the last approved package and
   compares obligation, implementation, policy, and evidence digests.
3. The auditor checks layer selection against the rubric below, including
   whether a materially different obligation received a materially different
   plan.
4. Evidence producers rerun only the invalidated or explicitly sampled checks;
   the auditor must not rewrite the business obligation to fit observed code.
5. The coordinator synthesizes coverage and findings. The independent auditor
   approves the decision, rejects it, or records `UNKNOWN` with a recovery plan.

#### 2.2 Verification-selection rubric

The coordinator selects layers by obligation property, not by ticket label,
file type, or preferred tool. Multiple layers are expected where they answer
different failure modes.

| Business obligation / risk | Most appropriate layer(s) | Why these layers are selected | Typical evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The business outcome, actor, scope, and non-goals are unambiguous | Business-process formalization; independent semantic audit | Formalization makes the obligation reviewable; an independent reader detects language that is technically precise but business-wrong | Approved obligation record, ambiguity decisions, reviewer sign-off |
| The verification plan covers the obligation and has an appropriate order | Verification planning; independent semantic audit | Planning checks completeness and dependencies; audit checks that the plan is not mechanically complete but substantively weak | Versioned plan, layer rationale, dependency graph, audit decision |
| The obligation is represented in the approved architecture and implementation seams | Engineering translation; implementation/conformance | Translation maps business language to design/code boundaries; conformance detects implementation outside the approved design | Design links, typed claims, conformance report, changed-surface map |
| Evidence still refers to the current obligation, code, policy, and environment | Linkage/freshness; change impact detection within the coordinator | Digest and freshness checks catch stale proof; impact analysis selects what must be rerun after change | Claim/content digests, validity window, invalidation report |
| User-visible behavior satisfies the acceptance outcome | Behavioral testing; independent semantic audit | Tests execute observable paths; an independent audit checks that the path actually represents the business outcome | Passing scenario results, boundary/error cases, screenshots or API assertions |
| A safety, invariant, or state property must hold for all modeled cases | Formal verification; implementation/conformance | Formal methods cover modeled state space where examples are insufficient; conformance checks connect the model to the shipped boundary | Model/checker result, assumptions, proof artifact, conformance mapping |
| Tests must detect a plausible incorrect implementation | Mutation testing; behavioral testing | Mutation survivors reveal weak assertions; behavioral tests provide the expected contract against which mutants are evaluated | Mutation score, survivor disposition, test results |
| The shipped artifact implements the approved design and policy | Implementation/conformance; linkage/freshness | Conformance checks structural and policy boundaries; freshness proves the checked artifact is the one being released | Build/revision digest, conformance report, release manifest |
| The complete evidence story is semantically credible and risks are acceptable | Independent semantic audit; all applicable producer layers | The audit is the final cross-layer check; it cannot substitute for missing producer evidence | Signed audit checklist, findings disposition, final coverage report |

Selection rules:

- Always select business-process formalization for a new or materially changed
  obligation.
- Always select linkage/freshness when evidence or implementation is reused
  across revisions.
- Select behavioral testing for an observable outcome unless the obligation is
  explicitly non-behavioral and the omission is justified.
- Select formal verification when the obligation contains an invariant,
  authorization rule, state-machine property, or exhaustive safety claim that
  can be modeled.
- Select mutation testing when the risk is that tests appear green while not
  checking the business rule, especially for limits, permissions, and failure
  handling.
- Select independent semantic audit for release-critical, high-impact, or
  disputed obligations, and whenever the coordinator detects contradictory or
  surprising evidence.

#### 2.3 Audit acceptance and recovery

The audit completes only when the package digest, selected-layer rationale,
coverage statuses, findings, and final decision are all recorded. A failed audit
creates a finding rather than silently reopening implementation work. Recovery
is to classify the gap, assign its owner, invalidate dependent evidence, and
return to the earliest entry path that can correct the intent: design for an
ambiguous obligation, planning for a missing layer, engineering translation for
an unrepresented rule, or implementation for a failed check.

### 3. Bootstrap/implementation entry path — making incomplete work reviewable

This path supports existing work that predates the package or arrives with
partial intent. It is an adoption path, not a lower standard. The coordinator
must make uncertainty visible and progressively close the gap before claiming
completion.

#### 3.1 Coordinator role and layer interfaces

The coordinator is a mechanically simple control-plane component. It owns
identity, ordering, policy enforcement, scheduling, state transitions, digests,
and evidence storage. It does not decide what business text means by matching
keywords or by treating a large change as inherently risky; semantic judgments
are explicit inputs from the business owner, reviewer, or a structured verifier.

Each layer has the same boundary contract:

| Interface field | Requirement |
| --- | --- |
| Input | Obligation/claim digest, source revision, policy snapshot, and relevant prior evidence |
| Output | Structured result, status, producer identity, environment, timestamps, assumptions, and evidence references |
| Failure | Typed outcome classified as `NOT_MET`, `UNKNOWN`, retryable, or non-retryable; no swallowed errors |
| Invalidation | Declared inputs whose change makes the result stale |
| Review | Human or automated approval role required before the result can affect completion |

The coordinator schedules the layers through the existing verification/run
infrastructure proposed by RDR-075. It must preserve mandatory host gates and
must not expose arbitrary implementation or prompt control as a substitute for
the typed interface.

#### 3.2 Evidence recording and change impact

Every evidence record binds at least:

- obligation and claim content digests;
- package and plan revision;
- source/release revision and relevant artifact digest;
- policy/configuration snapshot;
- producer, verifier version, environment, and execution time;
- result, assumptions, limitations, and linked findings; and
- retention, expiry, and re-run requirements.

On a change, the coordinator computes impact from these bindings and the
obligation-to-design, design-to-implementation, and implementation-to-evidence
links. It invalidates only what is affected when the dependency graph supports
that precision, but it may conservatively invalidate a larger set when impact
cannot be established. A changed business obligation always invalidates its
prior approval and dependent evidence; a documentation-only change may preserve
behavioral evidence when linkage/freshness proves that the executable and
policy inputs are unchanged.

Concrete example: changing `maximum refund is $500` to `$750` invalidates the
obligation digest, design translation, boundary tests, mutation results, and
final audit. It should not be treated as a copy edit merely because the code
diff is one line. Conversely, correcting a spelling error in a non-semantic
description can retain execution evidence if the package records and approves
the unchanged semantic digest.

#### 3.3 Adoption principles

1. **Start with the obligation.** Import existing requirements, then ask a
   business owner to confirm them before generating a plan.
2. **Make gaps first-class.** Use `UNKNOWN` and typed findings during migration;
   never manufacture green evidence to satisfy a rollout metric.
3. **Prefer small, reversible slices.** Adopt one capability or obligation
   family, measure stale evidence and unresolved findings, then expand.
4. **Keep layers replaceable.** A layer's contract and evidence shape are more
   durable than a particular test runner, model, or vendor.
5. **Preserve independence.** The author may explain evidence, but the final
   semantic audit belongs to a reviewer who can reject it.
6. **Do not weaken existing controls.** The add-on remains unavailable or
   non-blocking until its rollout guard, host-policy boundaries, and evidence
   behavior are validated as specified by RDR-075.
7. **Record the reason for every exception.** Exceptions expire and return to
   review; they are not a permanent alternate path.

#### 3.4 Bootstrap completion conditions

Bootstrap work is complete when the imported obligation set is owner-approved,
the selected plan is reviewed, each applicable layer has run or has an approved
inapplicability decision, evidence is claim-linked and fresh, and unresolved
findings have an explicit disposition. A package may be operationally adopted
with `UNKNOWN` findings only when the governing authority records an exception;
it may not report full completion or silently satisfy a blocking host policy.

## Alternatives Considered

### Alternative A: Treat the package as a side-channel artifact outside the coordinator

**Description**: Generate the review package as a documentation-only
post-process from the coordinator's structured output, without teaching the
coordinator to produce a digest, an approval record, or a completion
decision. The package is a Markdown export assembled at the end of a
release.

**Pros**: Smallest possible change to RDR-075's coordinator. Reuses
existing evidence and coverage records verbatim.

**Cons**: A side-channel package has no first-class identity, so digests
cannot bind, approvals cannot be versioned against a specific package
revision, and an audit cannot reliably reconstruct the package as it
existed at the time of approval. Two releases can produce the same
package content under different package digests and no reviewer can tell
the difference.

**Reason for rejection**: The package must be the same canonical object
the coordinator reasoned about; an export is a snapshot, not a contract.
The add-on teaches the coordinator to *produce* the package, not just
to be exportable into one.

### Alternative B: Keep RDR-075's six-category rubric

**Description**: Use the six risk categories as the selection rubric, and
add the package as the only new artifact.

**Pros**: No rubric rewrite; the package is the only new surface for a
reviewer to learn.

**Cons**: Six categories can collapse into a single layer set per
category, so a plan can be "complete" by the rubric yet leave a failure
mode uncovered. A rubric keyed to risk rather than to obligation property
also lets similar-risk obligations demand materially different layers
without the package making the difference visible.

**Reason for rejection**: The rubric is the lever that makes the
defense-in-depth posture reviewable. Nine complementary layers keyed to
obligation property, with explicit selection rules, is the minimum
change that makes a weak plan visible to a non-author.

### Alternative C: Drop the independent audit path

**Description**: Rely on the shared design path plus a single sign-off
step at release. Treat the audit as a side effect of the release review.

**Pros**: One fewer workflow; no audit-only state machine.

**Cons**: A reviewer who arrives after release — for a customer report,
an incident, a periodic review, or a follow-up feature — has no
explicit entry path. The release-time reviewer's mental model is not the
right model for an audit that starts from a release decision rather than
a design.

**Reason for rejection**: The independent audit path is the path that
makes a plausible-but-incomplete verification story detectable. Without
it, the package is an artifact of the design phase, not a durable
review surface.

### Alternative D: Bind the package to a single verifier (mutation only, or tests only)

**Description**: Pin the package to one verifier (e.g., mutation
testing) and require that verifier to produce the evidence index and the
coverage report.

**Pros**: No layer registry; no boundary contract to standardize.

**Cons**: Defense in depth requires multiple complementary layers; a
single verifier cannot both detect missing assertions and confirm the
business rule. The package would also have to follow the verifier's
schema, not the obligation's, which is the wrong default for a
business-facing review surface.

**Reason for rejection**: Defense in depth is the explicit design goal
of RDR-076. A single-verifier package cannot satisfy it.

## Trade-offs and Consequences

### Positive consequences

- **Reviewable by a non-author.** The package assembles obligation,
  plan, evidence, coverage, and decision into one document keyed to a
  content digest, so a reviewer who did not author the change can
  reconstruct the story.
- **Complementarity is visible.** Nine layers keyed to obligation
  property, with selection rules and rationale, make a weak plan
  detectable from the package alone.
- **Three entry paths share one surface.** The shared design, audit, and
  bootstrap paths all produce and consume the same package, so a
  reviewer can move between them without re-learning the artifact.
- **Defense in depth is enforced at the review surface.** No layer
  can clear the obligation alone; the host gates are a separate,
  mandatory section; missing evidence cannot be represented as `MET`.
- **Reuse without divergence.** The coordinator, the obligation
  model, the evidence model, and the host gates are RDR-075's; the
  add-on does not duplicate them.

### Negative consequences

- **RDR-076 is downstream of RDR-075.** The package, the rubric, and
  the audit workflow cannot ship until RDR-075's coordinator, obligation
  store, and evidence model exist. The 2026-10-12 readiness audit
  records that none of those have shipped; the umbrella #4365 remains
  open.
- **Two-step rollout.** RDR-075's coordinator and the RDR-076 package
  may need to ship in sequence, with the package unavailable (or
  non-blocking) until the coordinator is exercised in production.
- **Rubric change is a reviewer-facing change.** Operators and
  reviewers familiar with the six-category rubric will need to learn
  the nine-layer rubric. The audit report and the Implementation Plan
  must carry that transition explicitly.
- **Package overhead.** Every obligation set now produces a
  versioned, digitable package. Small, low-risk obligations still owe
  a minimal package; the bootstrap path keeps that lightweight.

### Risks and mitigations

- **Risk**: A reviewer can mark `MET` from a green test run without
  the package's other layers running.
  **Mitigation**: The completion decision is blocked by the package's
  validation rules; `MET` requires current, claim-linked evidence from
  every required layer and a recorded independent semantic audit.
- **Risk**: The package's host-gate section becomes a checkbox instead
  of a meaningful gate.
  **Mitigation**: The completion decision must record the host-gate
  state with timestamps; an empty host-gate section is itself a
  blocking finding.
- **Risk**: The nine-layer rubric collapses into the six-category rubric
  in practice.
  **Mitigation**: The selection rules (§ 2.2) name the *property* the
  layer verifies, not the risk category; the audit path explicitly
  checks that selection.
- **Risk**: An emergency release bypasses the package and the
  independent audit.
  **Mitigation**: The completion decision requires the audit's
  sign-off, and the existing emergency authority (RDR-075 § 1.5)
  requires an explicit exception, compensating control, expiry, and
  post-release review.
- **Risk**: The package's review surface drifts from the coordinator's
  structured output.
  **Mitigation**: The package digest is bound to the coordinator's
  evidence and coverage digests; a structural mismatch is a typed
  finding, not a warning.

## Rollout Guard

This RDR is a **docs-only** decision: it ships with the RDR-076 markdown
file, an update to this README's index, and the source-reference links. It
ships no runtime behavior, no schema, no model, no coordinator change, and
no code. There is no runtime rollout to guard, so the doc itself is the
deliverable and the standard `create_feature` allowlist is the only
shipping constraint.

- **Default state**: no runtime behavior changes. The `create_feature` run
  that produces this RDR is docs-only (per the docs-only allowlist enforced
  by `Features::RdrContract`); the implementation issues that consume
  this RDR ship behind RDR-075's layered-verification rollout guard,
  default off, with their own `## Rollout Guard` sections.
- **Enablement surface**: not applicable at the RDR-076 level. The
  package and the nine-layer rubric it describes are produced by the
  RDR-075 coordinator when the RDR-075 umbrella ships, behind that
  umbrella's rollout guard.
- **Rollback action**: revert this PR. The doc is the only artifact;
  the coordinator that produces the package ships under RDR-075's
  guard.
- **Cleanup criteria**: none for this RDR; the closeout criteria live
  in the RDR-075 umbrella and the `docs/intent/layered-verification/`
  EARS specs that implement the package.

If a future implementation issue materializes the package as code
(rather than as a docs-only decision), that implementation issue must
add its own `## Rollout Guard` section naming the relevant guard, its
default state, its enablement surface, its rollback action, and the
wiring that gates the runtime decision through the project's
appropriate guard mechanism.

## Implementation Plan

The implementation of RDR-076 is downstream of the RDR-075 implementation
umbrella (#4365) and the `docs/intent/layered-verification/` LID segment
that `lid_planning` will produce. This RDR does not produce code, schema, or
runtime behavior on its own; the implementation issues below describe the
shape the code must take *when* the umbrella is ready, and they are tentative
on EARS claim ids (`LAYER-VERIFY-###` is the working prefix advertised in
the LID segment) until the LID Planning PR lands.

### Phase 1: Review package structure

- Add a `Verification::ReviewPackage` value object with the eight parts in
  § 1.1 (obligation register, change boundary, verification plan, approval
  record, evidence index, coverage report, findings and exceptions,
  completion decision), bound to a content digest.
- Add a `Verification::ReviewPackages::Create` service that assembles a
  package from existing obligation, evidence, coverage, and finding records
  and computes the package digest.
- `@spec LAYER-VERIFY-001` (review package shape),
  `LAYER-VERIFY-002` (digest binding)

### Phase 2: Nine-layer selection rubric

- Replace RDR-075's six-category rubric with the nine-layer rubric in § 2.2,
  keyed to obligation *property*, with the selection rules in § 2.2 and the
  decision rules in § 1.3.
- Update `Verification::Rubric#select_layers(obligation)` to return the
  selected layer set with rationale, inapplicability decisions, and the
  mandatory host-gate surface (§ 1.3 rule 3).
- `@spec LAYER-VERIFY-003` (rubric rewrite),
  `LAYER-VERIFY-004` (rationale persistence)

### Phase 3: Independent audit path

- Add `Verification::Audits::Start` and `Verification::Audits::Complete`
  services that drive the workflow in § 2.1, import the last approved
  package, run the rubric check, and record the audit decision.
- Add an Inbox surface (`Inbox::VerificationAudit`) that surfaces pending
  audits and failed audits, distinct from the existing verification finding
  lanes.
- `@spec LAYER-VERIFY-005` (audit start),
  `LAYER-VERIFY-006` (audit complete)

### Phase 4: Coordinator contract for nine layers

- Standardize the layer boundary contract from § 3.1 (input, output, failure,
  invalidation, review) across all nine layers, so the package can treat
  evidence uniformly.
- `@spec LAYER-VERIFY-007` (boundary contract)

### Phase 5: Completion decision and host-gate surface

- Implement the completion decision state machine in § 1.5 (per
  exceptional path) plus the host-gate surface in § 1.3 rule 3.
- An empty host-gate section is a blocking finding; `MET` requires the
  host gates to be recorded and passing.
- `@spec LAYER-VERIFY-008` (completion decision),
  `LAYER-VERIFY-009` (host-gate surface)

### Phase 6: Bootstrap path integration

- Wire the bootstrap completion conditions in § 3.4 into the coordinator
  so a bootstrap-entered obligation set reports `UNKNOWN` and an explicit
  recovery plan until each applicable layer has run or has an approved
  inapplicability decision.
- `@spec LAYER-VERIFY-010`

### Closeout

- RDR-076 closeout/validation issue following

## Validation

### Testing approach

1. Unit: `Verification::ReviewPackage` assembles the eight parts from
   stub obligation, evidence, coverage, and finding records; the digest is
   stable for identical inputs and changes when any part changes.
2. Unit: `Verification::Rubric#select_layers` returns the nine-layer set
   keyed to obligation property, with rationale and inapplicability
   decisions, and refuses to omit a layer without a recorded decision.
3. Integration: a shared-design entry produces an approved package;
   later changes invalidate only the affected parts; the completion
   decision blocks while a host gate is missing.
4. Integration: an independent audit reconstructs or imports the last
   approved package, runs the rubric check, records the decision, and
   never rewrites the obligation.
5. Integration: a bootstrap-entered obligation set reports `UNKNOWN`
   with a recovery plan; the bootstrap completion conditions gate the
   completion decision.
6. End-to-end: a defensible plan with two complementary layers per
   failure mode clears the rubric, the audit, and the host-gate check
   and reaches `MET`; a weak plan (single layer per failure mode) is
   rejected by the rubric.

### Acceptance criteria

1. A `ReviewPackage` exposes the eight parts with digests and a package
   digest; identical inputs produce identical digests.
2. `Rubric#select_layers` returns the nine-layer set keyed to obligation
   property, records rationale and inapplicability decisions, and refuses
   to omit a mandatory layer without a justified decision.
3. The completion decision is blocked when any required host gate is
   missing; an empty host-gate section is a blocking finding.
4. The independent audit path produces a signed audit decision linked
   to a specific package revision; the audit never rewrites the
   obligation to fit observed code.
5. Bootstrap-entered obligation sets report `UNKNOWN` with a recovery
   plan; the bootstrap completion conditions (§ 3.4) gate the completion
   decision.
6. Layer boundary contract (§ 3.1) is enforced for all nine layers; a
   layer that cannot bind the input digests, environment, policy
   snapshot, and validity window produces `UNKNOWN`, not `MET`.
7. The package's review surface shares one structure across all three
   entry paths; a reviewer can move between paths without re-learning
   the artifact.

### Test scenarios

1. **Scenario**: A payment-limit obligation is authored through the
   shared-design path; the rubric selects behavioral testing, mutation
   testing, implementation conformance, and an independent semantic
   audit; all evidence is current and linked.
   **Expected**: The package reaches `MET`; the completion decision
   records all four layers, the host gates, and the audit decision.
2. **Scenario**: A reviewer arrives after release and starts an
   independent audit on the same obligation set.
   **Expected**: The audit reconstructs the last approved package,
   checks the rubric, runs invalidated checks only, and records the
   audit decision without rewriting the obligation.
3. **Scenario**: A bootstrap-entered obligation set imports a partially
   defined obligation and selects all nine layers.
   **Expected**: The package reports `UNKNOWN` per obligation with a
   recovery plan; the completion decision is blocked until the bootstrap
   completion conditions are met.
4. **Scenario**: An engineer marks a failed mutation test as resolved
   by changing the prompt.
   **Expected**: The original finding remains; a plan revision and
   fresh approval are required before the package may reach `MET`.
5. **Scenario**: The host-gate section is empty because CI is failing.
   **Expected**: The completion decision is blocked; the package's
   completion decision reports the missing gate, not `MET`.
6. **Scenario**: The nine-layer rubric is reduced to a six-layer
   category rubric in a code change.
   **Expected**: The audit path flags the change as a rubric regression;
   the rubric rewrite is reverted or explicitly approved with rationale.

### Desired user outcome

A reviewer who did not author a change can read the review package and
answer, without opening source code, what the obligation is, what layers
verify it, what evidence proves it, who approved the plan, what remains
unresolved, and whether the release decision is permitted to proceed. The
nine-layer rubric makes the defense-in-depth posture reviewable; the
independent audit path makes a plausible-but-incomplete verification
story detectable; the host-gate surface makes the host-policy contract
visible at the completion decision.
